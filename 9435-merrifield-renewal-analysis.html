The situation

Your residents are approaching the end of their second year at $3,420, with no increase in either year. You offered to hold the rent flat again. They have countered at $3,160 — a reduction of $260 per month. Their lease ends November 26, 2026.

This analysis answers two questions: what the home would actually rent for today, and what each option costs you.

We reviewed 22 comparable rentals in Laureate Park and Lake Nona — every three and four-bedroom home between 1,776 and 2,019 square feet, active and leased, over the past several months.

Not one of them has a private pool. Not one has an outdoor kitchen. None has anything comparable to the glass garage-style door that opens the living room to the patio.

That matters more than it would in most communities. Laureate Park is built at high density with small lots, and private pools are genuinely rare here. A renter who wants a pool in this neighborhood has very few places to go — and the ones that exist are typically larger, more expensive homes.

A note on how we valued the pool

We used $300 a month for the private pool, and we want to explain that figure rather than leave it as an assertion.

In most Central Florida markets a private pool adds somewhere between $150 and $300 a month. We used the top of that range here for one reason: scarcity. Across 22 comparable Laureate Park rentals, there is no competing product. A renter who wants a pool in this community cannot simply choose a different listing — there isn't one at this size or price.

Scarcity is what turns an amenity into leverage. The outdoor kitchen and the glass patio door are valued conservatively at $75 and $50, because while they are genuinely unusual, they appeal to a narrower set of renters than a pool does.

A single vacant month costs $3,420 — more than the entire year's concession of $3,120. And that is before make-ready, cleaning, any paint or carpet, and the cost of placing a new resident.

The break-even is 27 days. If losing these residents costs you more than 27 days of vacancy, accepting the reduction was the better financial decision — and that calculation ignores turn costs entirely.

And the timing works against us

The lease ends November 26 — the day before Thanksgiving, at the front edge of the slowest rental stretch of the year.

That cuts both ways, and we want to be honest about both.

  • In your favor: most households do not want to move during the holidays. There is real inertia working for a renewal.
  • Against you: if they do leave, the home comes to market in December and January, when the renter pool is at its smallest. The two single-family homes that took longest to lease this year — 41 and 49 days — did so in summer. Winter would likely be slower.

Section Five

Reading the counter

We cannot know their reasoning, and they are not going to show us their hand. But it is worth understanding the context they are almost certainly operating in.

There is a great deal of commentary circulating right now — much of it on social media — that rents have flattened or fallen and that this is a renter's market, and that anyone renewing should counter their landlord. Some of that is true in the aggregate. Central Florida rents have softened.

What that general advice does not account for is a home like this one. Softening applies to interchangeable product. Your home is not interchangeable in this community.

If the pool, the outdoor kitchen and the indoor-outdoor living are part of why they stayed two years, they will struggle to replace them in Laureate Park at any price near $3,160. If they have decided those things do not matter to them, that is a different conversation — but they are unlikely to tell us which it is.

What we do know is that they countered rather than giving notice. Residents who intend to leave generally leave. Residents who counter are usually trying to stay on better terms.

Section Six

Our recommendation

Counter at $3,300 for an 18-month term

This is the option we would take, and the term matters as much as the number.

$3,300 meets them meaningfully — a $120 reduction, and inside the market range the comparables support. It costs you $1,440 over twelve months, less than half of one vacant month.

An 18-month term moves the lease end to late May 2028 — peak rental season — instead of late November. If you are going to concede on rent, buying a better lease-end month is the most valuable thing you can get in return. It is worth far more than the $120 difference.

If they hold firm at $3,160, take it — but ask for the term

$3,160 costs you $3,120 across the year. One vacant month costs $3,420, and a December vacancy would likely run longer than that. Accepting is still the better financial outcome than risking a winter turnover.

If you accept their number, ask for the 18-month term in exchange. That gives you something real for the concession and moves the next renewal into a much stronger season.

What we would not do

We would not hold firm at $3,420 on principle. The market supports that rent — our analysis says it does — but proven residents who pay on time and care for a home are worth more than the last $260, particularly with the lease ending the day before Thanksgiving.

One more thing worth weighing. These residents have been in place nearly two years with no increase, which means the rent has effectively been declining in real terms for them as well. They are not asking for something unreasonable — they are responding to a market narrative. A modest counter with a longer term acknowledges that without giving away the full $260.
